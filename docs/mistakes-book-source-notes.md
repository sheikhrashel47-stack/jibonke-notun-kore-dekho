# THE MISTAKES THAT COST US OUR LIVES — Evidence Notes

## Editorial rule

This book separates illustrative composite cases from verified real-world cases. A composite story is labelled explicitly as a fictionalized composite example. Public figures, companies, historical decisions, experiments, and quantitative claims are used only when the source is named and linked. Psychology language is qualified when the evidence is mixed or the concept is debated. Money chapters are educational and do not recommend a specific investment product.

## Verified research anchors

### Cognitive bias and decision-making

Berthet’s review in *Frontiers in Psychology* synthesizes research on cognitive biases in professional decision-making across management, finance, medicine, and law. It defines anchoring, availability, confirmation, hindsight, outcome, overconfidence, and framing effects, while also warning that the evidence varies by domain and that vignette findings may have limited ecological validity. This supports teaching bias concepts as tendencies and decision prompts, not as deterministic diagnoses.

Source: Vincent Berthet, “The Impact of Cognitive Biases on Professionals’ Decision-Making: A Review of Four Occupational Areas,” *Frontiers in Psychology* 12 (2022), PMCID PMC8763848. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8763848/

### Habit formation and the myth of 21 days

Singh, Murphy, Maher, and Smith’s 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis examined 20 studies involving 2,601 participants. The review reported substantial individual variability in time to habit formation, with median or mean estimates in the included studies ranging from roughly two months to several months, and individual times ranging widely. Most included studies had a high risk-of-bias rating. Therefore the book must not promise that any habit forms in exactly 21 days; it should emphasize repetition, stable context, self-selected behavior, and variability.

Source: Ben Singh et al., “Time to Form a Habit: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Health Behaviour Habit Formation and Its Determinants,” *Healthcare* 12(23) (2024), PMCID PMC11641623. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/

### Digital behavior-change interventions

Zhu et al.’s 2024 systematic review of 41 articles on digital behavior-change interventions reported frequent use of self-monitoring, goal-setting, prompts/cues, automatic monitoring, feedback, self-set goals, time-based cues, and virtual rewards. The review also notes challenges with long-term adoption and the limits of interventions that depend on continual user initiative. The book can use these as design ideas, but must not imply that apps guarantee durable change.

Source: Yujie Zhu et al., “Digital Behavior Change Intervention Designs for Habit Formation: Systematic Review,” *Journal of Medical Internet Research* 26 (2024), e54375. https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e54375/

### Financial literacy, budgeting, and financial well-being

Bai’s PLOS One study examined financial literacy, mental budgeting, self-control, investment decision-making, and subjective financial well-being in a sample of 449 Chinese university students using PLS-SEM. The study reported positive associations among these variables, but its population, design, and statistical model limit broad causal conclusions. The money section should use this as one research anchor, not as proof that budgeting alone determines financial outcomes.

Source: Ruofan Bai, “Impact of financial literacy, mental budgeting and self control on financial wellbeing: Mediating impact of investment decision making,” *PLOS One* 18(11) (2023), PMCID PMC10645357. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10645357/

## Case-study taxonomy

| Label | Meaning | Required treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Verified real case | A public event or study with a reliable source | Name the source, preserve uncertainty, do not dramatize unsupported details |
| Research example | An experiment, review, or dataset | State population/design/limitation where relevant |
| Fictionalized composite | An illustrative story built from common patterns | Label clearly; never present as a real person or event |
| Reader scenario | A hypothetical self-check prompt | Keep short and avoid diagnosis |

## Claim-strength labels

Use **evidence-supported**, **plausible mechanism**, **illustrative**, or **uncertain/contested** where a reader could otherwise mistake a teaching explanation for a settled scientific fact.

## No-blame guardrail

A personal error can be shaped by fear, habit, knowledge gaps, health, family, class, discrimination, opportunity, timing, and environment. The book teaches agency without claiming that every outcome is fully controlled by the individual.
