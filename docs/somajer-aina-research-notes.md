# সমাজের আয়না — গবেষণা নোট

## World Bank: Bangladesh country page

Source: World Bank, “Bangladesh,” https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/bangladesh

Extracted 21 August 2026. The page reports that in 2023 Bangladesh’s net primary enrollment was 93.1% and lower-secondary enrollment was 76.3%, with higher enrollment for girls than boys. It also reports that agriculture employs nearly half of the population, that social protection programmes reached 14.4 million beneficiaries, and that digital payments were supported for 12 million vulnerable people. Health and development improvements are also documented, including reductions in maternal mortality, under-five mortality, and stunting.

Use: education access, gendered enrollment, agriculture and work, social protection, and the distinction between progress and unequal lived experience. Do not treat these figures as a complete description of educational quality, income distribution, or individual opportunity. Cite the original World Bank page in the final manuscript.

## UNICEF Bangladesh: Education

Source: UNICEF Bangladesh, “Education,” https://www.unicef.org/bangladesh/en/education

Extracted 21 August 2026. UNICEF reports that only 19% of children aged 3–5 attend an early childhood education programme; only 64% continue to complete secondary education; children with disabilities are seven times more likely to be out of school than other children; married girls are more than four times more likely to be out of school than unmarried peers; and nearly 20 million children are seriously exposed to climate hazards that can disrupt education. UNICEF also reports that fewer than one-third of children aged 3–5 are on track for literacy and numeracy goals, that 43% of 10-year-olds were proficient readers before COVID-19, and that 25% of secondary graduates attained basic competencies. These figures are programme-page claims and should be presented with the date and source context rather than as timeless facts.

Use: education inequality, access versus learning quality, disability and marriage-related exclusion, climate disruption, and the need to distinguish enrollment from learning outcomes.

## ILOSTAT: Bangladesh labour-market profile

Source: ILOSTAT, “Bangladesh country profile,” https://ilostat.ilo.org/data/country-profiles/bgd/

The profile identifies Bangladesh as Southern Asia and lower-middle income, with the latest household survey listed as the Labour Force Survey 2023 (2023 Q4) and standards based on the 19th ICLS. The page explains that figures may be national or ILO modelled estimates, and that missing values or different standards must be interpreted with care. It defines youth NEET as the share of youth aged 15–24 not in employment, education, or training, and notes that the profile covers labour-force participation, employment, underutilization, working conditions, social protection, and occupational safety. Because the interactive page was captcha-blocked during retrieval, use the profile URL for verification and do not invent indicator values that were not accessible in the page text.

Use: explain why labour statistics require definitions, survey dates, and methodological caveats; support the book’s distinction between unemployment, underemployment, and NEET status.

## American Psychological Association: adolescent social media advisory

Source: American Psychological Association, “Health advisory on social media use in adolescence,” https://www.apa.org/topics/social-media-internet/health-advisory-adolescent-social-media-use

The advisory states that social-media use is not inherently beneficial or harmful; effects depend on the adolescent’s characteristics, social circumstances, content, platform features, and offline life. It cautions that causal evidence is limited, long-term research is often unavailable, and marginalized populations are underrepresented in the evidence base. It also notes that potential risks may be greater in early adolescence and that algorithms and platform design can shape what users see. These qualifications are important: the book should avoid both “social media is always harmful” and “technology is neutral” claims.

Use: curated identity, validation, comparison, algorithms, attention, misinformation, and balanced media literacy guidance.

## OECD: social mobility and equal opportunity

Source: OECD, “Social mobility and equal opportunity,” https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/social-mobility-and-equal-opportunity.html

The OECD defines social mobility as movement in socioeconomic position relative to one’s parents or across a person’s lifetime. This provides a useful distinction between individual effort, inherited advantage, institutional access, and intergenerational change. The final book should use this concept carefully and avoid implying that every outcome can be explained by either merit or privilege alone.

Use: social class, opportunity inequality, inherited wealth, education, networks, privilege, and nuanced discussion of meritocracy.
